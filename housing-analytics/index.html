Buying

Every borough fails the old rule of thumb that a home should cost four to five times a salary. The question is by how much, and what that means for someone trying to buy.

Q1Which boroughs are least affordable when prices are compared with local pay?

Kensington and Chelsea, by a distance. An average home there costs 23.3 times the median full-time salary of the people who live there. The cheapest boroughs relative to pay, Tower Hamlets and Croydon, still sit at 8.8 times.

The ratio divides the average house price in each borough by the median gross annual pay of full-time employees who live there, wherever they work. That is the right lens for local affordability: it asks whether residents could buy where they live.

Westminster (16.6×) and Hammersmith and Fulham (15.4×) follow Kensington and Chelsea. The middle of the table is tightly bunched: half of all boroughs sit between about 10.5× and 13.5×. Inner London's median is 13.0×, against 11.3× in Outer London.

Low ratios in Tower Hamlets and Newham are partly a mix effect: most homes sold there are flats, which pull the average price down. Q4 separates property type from place.

Q2How far does a typical 10% deposit sit from local earnings?

Between 10.5 and 28 months of gross pay. Few people can save their whole salary, so at a more realistic 15% saving rate that becomes 6 to 16 years, with 7.9 years in the median borough.

This is the hidden barrier. Even where mortgage payments might be manageable, the deposit is a multi-year project, and rent is being paid the whole time. Move the slider to see how saving rate and deposit size change the wait.

Q3Can a typical earner get a big enough mortgage?

Not on one salary. Most lenders cap borrowing at about 4.5 times income. On that basis, a single median earner cannot reach the average home in any borough, even with a 10% deposit. Two median earners can reach 6 boroughs, and an average flat in 25.

The calculator sets your buying budget as income × lending multiple, plus the deposit. It then compares that budget with the average price in each borough, for the property type you choose. The monthly repayment uses the interest rate you set. The default of 5.95% is the average two-year fixed rate in November 2026 (Moneyfacts), up from 4.83% earlier in the year. At the same monthly payment, that rise alone cuts how much you can borrow by about 12%.

Q4Does property type matter more than location?

Often, yes. Across boroughs, the median flat costs 8.4 times local pay, a terraced house 13.3×, a semi-detached 17.2× and a detached house 26.7×. Choosing a flat over a house usually changes affordability more than moving borough does.

Flats are under 10 times pay in 25 of 33 boroughs, and the cheapest relative to pay are in the outer east and south: Havering (5.5×), Bexley (5.5×) and Croydon (5.7×). At the other end, a detached house in Kensington and Chelsea averages £4.4m, which is 83.5 times local pay.

Renting

Renting is the default for younger Londoners and is no cheap alternative. Rents have risen faster than house prices for a decade, and the gap with pay only began to narrow in 2025.

Q5How much of a full-time salary does rent take?

About half, and more than 30% everywhere. The average private rent takes 39% to 83% of a borough's median full-time gross pay, with 52% in the median borough. All 32 boroughs with rent data are above the common 30% affordability benchmark.

Inner London is the most stretched, at 60% of pay against 46% in Outer London. Kensington and Chelsea (83%), Westminster (72%) and Hammersmith and Fulham (69%) top the list. Bromley (39%), Havering (41%) and Croydon (42%) are the least stretched, but still well above 30%. These figures use gross pay. After tax, the share is higher still.

Q6Where is rental pressure rising faster than local income growth?

In 18 of 32 boroughs, led by outer east London. Rents in Bexley rose 9.7 percentage points faster than local pay, and in Barking and Dagenham 8.6 points faster. In several prime inner boroughs, rents are now falling.

The chart subtracts each borough's annual pay growth from its annual rent growth. Above zero means rents are pulling away from what residents earn. Read the extremes with care. Borough-level pay comes from a sample survey, so a single year's change can be noisy. Camden's pay rose 19.1% in the survey, which is why it looks like the borough with the least rental pressure. Boroughs marked with an asterisk have less precise pay estimates.

Q7Have rents or house prices risen more since 2015?

Rents, in every borough. Since January 2015, the median borough's rent is up 47.5% and its average house price 31.7%. Outer London rents grew most: Barking and Dagenham up 75%, Havering and Bexley up 70%.

Every point in the chart sits above the diagonal, where rents and prices would have grown equally. Prime central London shows the starkest split. Prices in Westminster (−16%), Kensington and Chelsea (−14%) and Hammersmith and Fulham (−4%) are lower than in 2015, while rents there rose 30%, 36% and 42%. For renters hoping to switch to buying, the deposit has got cheaper relative to rent, but saving it from a larger rent bill has got harder.

Q8Is rent inflation cooling?

It was, sharply, until early 2026. Annual rent growth peaked at 11.2% in Inner London in January 2025 and 10.7% in Outer London in October 2024. By February 2026 it was down to 1.8% and 4.1%, against London pay growth of 3.6%.

That made Inner London the first part of the city in years where rents were rising more slowly than pay. The relief may be short-lived. Newer ONS releases, after this dataset ends, show London rent growth picking up again to about 3.5% by September 2026, as landlords leave the market and new regulation and tax rises arrive. The outlook page covers why.

The market

London prices have barely moved in cash terms for nearly a decade, while pay and rents have kept rising. That slow, quiet correction is the main story of the market.

Q9Are house prices falling?

In cash terms, slightly. Against pay, clearly. The median borough's average price fell 1.9% in the year to February 2026, while median pay rose 3.6%. Prime central London is still well below its peak: the City is 31.7% below its all-time high, Westminster 28.8% and Kensington and Chelsea 26.0%.

Over 2015–2025, Inner London prices grew just 0.9% a year, against 2.8% in Outer London. Nine boroughs are more than 10% below their record price. At the other end, Barking and Dagenham, Sutton and Redbridge are at or within half a percent of theirs. Because these figures are not adjusted for inflation, they understate the real fall. Prices that are flat in cash terms are falling in real terms every year.

Q10How do sales volumes and prices move together?

Sales move first. London recorded 77,988 sales in 2025, less than half the 173,993 of 2002. In the same year, changes in sales and prices are only weakly linked (r = 0.17). But this year's change in sales is a much better guide to next year's change in prices (r = 0.53).

That makes transaction volume a useful early warning. Sales fell 5.5% in 2025 after a brief rebound in 2024, which points to soft prices in 2026. That is what has happened so far: HM Land Registry's July 2026 figure put London prices 3.3% lower than a year earlier. Every borough is now 23% to 50% below its 2021 sales level, when the stamp duty holiday pulled purchases forward.

Q11Inner or Outer London: which is winning?

Outer London, for over a decade. At its 2014 peak, a typical Inner London home cost 1.72 times one in Outer London. That premium has since shrunk to about 1.3×, back to roughly where it was in the early 2000s.

Several forces have pushed in the same direction: tax changes that hit expensive and second homes hardest, weaker overseas demand for prime central property, and a lasting shift towards space and gardens after 2020. Outer boroughs such as Barking and Dagenham, Havering and Bexley grew about 4% a year over 2015–2025. Kensington and Chelsea and Westminster went slightly backwards.

Investors and risk

Landlords weigh rents against prices. Prices have stalled while rents have climbed, so rental yields are the highest in the decade of data, but so are borrowing costs.

Q12Where are rental yields highest?

In the cheaper east London boroughs. Gross yields reach 6.3% in Tower Hamlets and 6.0% in Newham, against 3.4% in Richmond and 3.5% in Kensington and Chelsea. The London median has risen from 3.3% in 2022 to 4.3%.

Cheaper boroughs pay higher yields (the correlation between yield and price is −0.44), because rents vary less across London than prices do. These are gross figures, before mortgage interest, letting fees, maintenance, empty periods and tax. With two-year fixed mortgage rates near 6% in late 2026, a buy-to-let bought mostly with borrowed money in a 4% yield borough loses money before any price growth. That is one reason landlords are selling up.

Q13Which boroughs have the most volatile prices?

Prime central London. Monthly price changes vary most in the City of London, Westminster and Kensington and Chelsea. Havering, Hillingdon and Bromley are the steadiest.

Volatility here is the standard deviation of monthly price changes since January 2015. The City's figure partly reflects how few homes sell there, so one large sale can move the average. Westminster and Kensington and Chelsea are volatile because their buyers are more sensitive to tax changes, currency moves and global wealth. Those are the same boroughs that have fallen furthest from their peaks.

Q14Where is overall housing stress greatest?

Kensington and Chelsea, Hounslow and Westminster. The housing stress index combines how expensive it is to buy, how expensive it is to rent, and how fast rents have risen over five years. Bromley, Newham and Croydon score lowest.

Each of the three measures is turned into a z-score (how far a borough is from the London average, in standard deviations), and the three are averaged with equal weight. It is a ranking tool, not a forecast. Hounslow ranks highly not because it is the most expensive, but because its rents have grown fast relative to its residents' pay.

Where is the market heading?

Our data points to a slow correction measured against pay, not a crash in prices. Events since February 2026 have shifted the near-term picture, and the outlook page covers them in full.

The short version: expect flat-to-lower London prices into 2027, led by prime central boroughs, with outer London steadier. Expect rents to keep rising at about the pace of pay or faster. Affordability improves only slowly, because pay grows while prices stand still.

  • Momentum is fading. The share of boroughs with rising prices fell from 82% in December 2024 to 45% in February 2026. Price growth slowed over the previous six months in 30 of 33 boroughs.
  • Sales point the same way. Volumes fell 5.5% in 2025. Historically, falling sales come before softer prices the next year (Q10).
  • Borrowing got dearer. A war in the Middle East pushed up energy prices and inflation. The Bank of England raised Bank Rate to 4% in November 2026, and two-year fixed mortgages now average about 5.95%.
  • Rents are back on the rise. Landlords face higher taxes from April 2027 and new tenant protections from May 2026. Fewer homes to rent tends to mean higher rents.
  • Pay is doing the work. If pay keeps rising about 3.6% a year and prices stay flat, the median price-to-earnings ratio falls from 11.9× to about 10× by 2031.

How the numbers work

Sources

  • House prices and sales: HM Land Registry UK House Price Index, covering average prices, sales volumes and prices by property type for each borough from January 1995.
  • Rents: ONS Price Index of Private Rents (PIPR), average monthly private rent by borough from January 2015.
  • Pay: ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings (ASHE) 2025 provisional, Table 8: median gross annual pay for full-time employees by place of residence, with coefficients of variation.
  • Boundaries: ONS Open Geography Portal, local authority districts (May 2024).

Rules the pipeline follows

  • No mixing of eras. Pay is attached only to 2025–26 months, so 1990s prices are never divided by today's salaries. A dbt test enforces this.
  • Precision travels with the data. ASHE coefficients of variation are mapped to ONS precision bands, and less precise borough pay estimates are flagged in the charts.
  • City of London. ONS suppresses its pay estimate because the sample is too small, so the London-wide figure is used. PIPR publishes no rent series for it.
  • Inner and Outer London follow the ONS statistical definition: 14 inner and 19 outer authorities.
  • Prices are nominal (not inflation-adjusted). Borough HPI is a 3-month moving average, and recent sales counts are revised up as registrations arrive.
  • Gross yield is average rent ÷ average price across all homes. It is indicative, before costs, empty periods and tax.
