Growth is holding up, but inflation is rising again, and the Bank of England has started raising rates. For housing, the cost of a mortgage matters most.

The key point: a healthy job market limits forced sales, so a crash is unlikely. But dearer mortgages shrink what buyers can pay. The market adjusts through fewer sales and slowly falling prices, not panic selling. That is exactly the pattern of 2023, when sales fell 29% and prices fell less than 1%.

The Middle East and energy: how a war reaches a London mortgage

In March 2026, after US and Israeli strikes on Iran, Iran brought shipping through the Strait of Hormuz to a standstill. The International Energy Agency called it the largest disruption to global energy supplies in history.

Oil went above $100 a barrel in October. In its November forecast, the Bank of England assumed oil would peak at about $116 a barrel and wholesale gas at 215p a therm in the final quarter of 2026. Markets have since eased: Brent was about $89 on 24 November, a three-month low, as other supply returns. But tankers were still hitting mines in the strait that week. The risk has not gone away.

The energy shock hurts London twice. It pushes buyers out of the market through mortgage rates and pushes rents up through landlord costs. The two channels work against each other for prices, which is why the most likely result is stagnation rather than a sharp fall.

Trade and US tariffs

London is mostly a services economy, so US tariffs matter less to it directly than energy does. Their main effect on housing is through confidence and the wider UK growth outlook.

  • Most UK goods still face a 10% US tariff on top of normal duties. After the US Supreme Court struck down some tariffs on 20 February 2026, the US said it would honour existing bilateral deals, so little changed for most UK exporters.
  • Steel faces 25%, against 50% for most other countries. The promised quota has not been finalised, and since April 2026 the lower rate has required 95% UK-origin metal.
  • Pharmaceuticals secured the clearest win. A 2 April 2026 deal means no US tariffs on UK medicines and medical technology until 19 January 2029, in return for higher NHS spending on new drugs.
  • The OBR cut its 2026 UK growth forecast to 1.1% in March, before the Middle East escalation, and listed trade policy among the main risks.

For prime central London, the bigger issue is global wealth and sentiment. Volatile markets, a strong or weak pound, and tax changes all move overseas demand. That demand has been falling for a decade, as the shrinking Inner London premium shows.

Why rents are likely to keep rising

Rent growth cooled sharply through 2025 and then picked up again in 2026. The economics of being a landlord explain why.

For a landlord buying with a mortgage, the sums no longer work. London's median gross yield is 4.3%, before any costs, while a two-year fixed mortgage costs about 5.95%. A buy-to-let with a 75% mortgage now pays more in interest than it earns in rent before costs.

  • Supply is shrinking. Negative returns on borrowed money, higher tax on rental income from April 2027, and new tenant rules all push smaller landlords to sell, and few new investors are replacing them.
  • Demand is rising. Higher mortgage rates keep would-be first-time buyers renting for longer.
  • But pay caps it. Rent already takes 52% of a median full-time salary. Rents cannot outgrow pay for long when they already take that much, which is why the 2024 peak of 10–11% faded so fast.

Our reading: London rents will probably grow about 3–5% in 2027, roughly the pace of pay or a little faster. Outer boroughs with the highest yields (Barking and Dagenham, Newham, Tower Hamlets) have the most room to rise. Prime inner boroughs already ask more than residents can pay.

Three scenarios for 2027

What happens next depends mainly on energy prices, and so on how far the Bank of England has to go. Each scenario names the conditions that would make it true.

Central: a slow grind lower

The most likely path

Oil settles at $80–95, inflation peaks near 5% in mid-2027, and Bank Rate tops out at about 4.25%. Mortgage rates stay at 5.5–6% through 2027.

  • London prices about 3.5% lower in 2027, in line with Savills' November forecast
  • Prime central London weakest
  • Recovery of about 3% a year from 2028

Downside: the energy shock deepens

Less likely, the main risk

The Strait of Hormuz stays shut, oil goes back above $110, and inflation stays over 5%. Bank Rate rises to 4.5–5%, and mortgage rates rise past 6.5%.

  • London prices 6–8% lower in 2027, flat in 2028
  • Sales fall back towards the 2023 low of about 71,000
  • Rents keep rising as more would-be buyers rent

Upside: quick de-escalation

Less likely

A lasting reopening of Hormuz sends oil below $80. Inflation falls back in 2027, and the Bank reverses the November rise. Mortgage rates ease to about 4.5–5%.

  • London prices flat in 2027, up 3–4% a year after that
  • Pent-up buyers return, lifting sales first (see Q10)
  • Rent growth cools as some renters buy

In every scenario

What does not change

Pay keeps growing about 3–4% a year. As long as prices rise more slowly than that, London becomes a little more affordable each year. That has been happening since about 2016.

  • Outer London keeps outperforming Inner London
  • Flats remain the only option within reach for most single buyers

Which boroughs are most exposed?

Different shocks hit different parts of London. These lists are computed from the borough data to February 2026.

Boroughs show up on more than one list for a reason. Kensington and Chelsea and Westminster face higher mortgage costs, the £2m surcharge and volatile overseas demand together. The resilient group of outer boroughs is cheaper relative to pay and still had rising prices at the start of 2026, so dearer mortgages hurt them less.

What it means for you

  • First-time buyers: prices are unlikely to run away from you in 2027, and slowly falling prices mean the deposit target stops moving. The constraint is the mortgage rate, not the price. Flats in outer east and south London are within reach of two median earners (Q3). Your First Home may help with new-builds from spring 2027.
  • Homeowners moving: if you sell and buy in the same market, falling prices matter less than the rate on your new mortgage. Fixed rates near 6% make the size of the loan the main cost.
  • Renters: expect rent rises of about 3–5% a year, now limited to one a year by law. You are better protected against no-fault eviction since May 2026.
  • Landlords: borrowing costs more than gross yields in most boroughs, and tax rises from April 2027. Only higher-yield boroughs, or owning without a mortgage, still work on rental income alone.
  • Policymakers: pay growing faster than prices is doing the slow work of restoring affordability. The rental market is where pressure is building.

This is general analysis of public data, not personal financial advice. Speak to a qualified adviser before making decisions about buying, selling or borrowing.
